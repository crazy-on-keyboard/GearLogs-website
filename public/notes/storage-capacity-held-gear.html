Field Note FN-010 · Inventory

The shelf has a ceiling

Why gear in someone’s hands still takes up room, and what to count before you order more.

The radio shelf is empty on Monday, so the storeman orders forty more. On Wednesday the field exercise ends and sixty radios come back through the door. There is room for thirty-two. The rest spend a month in cardboard boxes on the floor, which is where two of them get trodden on.

Nothing about that story is careless. He counted the shelf and the shelf was empty. The mistake is older than the storeman: an empty shelf looks like free room, and it almost never is. Most of what a store-room owns is not on the shelf on any given day. It is on people, in vehicles, at the repair bench, in a locker two buildings away. All of it is coming back.

Stock is what you can touch. Held is what you are responsible for. A shelf has a physical ceiling — forty radios fit, forty-one do not — and that ceiling applies to everything that will one day return to it, not to whatever happens to be sitting there this morning. If you own sixty radios and the shelf takes forty, you are over. It does not matter that fifty-five of them are out.

So the honest count has three parts. What is in stock. What is in use, signed out to someone. What is away for repair. Add them up and that is what you hold. Subtract that from the ceiling and you have your headroom: the number you may actually order. A store that tracks only the first part will overbuy every quiet week and run out of floor every busy one.

An empty shelf is not room. Room is what nobody is holding.

One item leaves the sum, and it is worth being precise about it. Gear you have written off — lost, stolen, condemned, not coming back — is no longer held, and it frees room the moment you record it. That is a good reason to write things off promptly instead of leaving them “out” for a year in the hope they turn up. The opposite is also true: if a written-off item does turn up and you recover it, it takes its room back. The ceiling should follow the truth, in both directions.

The second thing worth deciding is what the ceiling is for. It is tempting to make it a wall: the system refuses the forty-first radio, and the storeman with sixty in the doorway now has a fight with a screen. That helps nobody. The radios exist. Refusing to record them does not make them fit; it makes the record wrong, which is worse than a full room. A ceiling is a signal for a decision — order less, find a second shelf, write off the eight that will never work again — not a lock on the door.

The useful shape is a soft ceiling with a question. Register the units and the system tells you plainly that this puts you over, by how much, and asks whether to record them anyway. The answer is nearly always yes, because the gear is real. But the question was asked, the number was seen, and the person who orders things now knows the store is at one hundred and forty-five percent instead of finding out from the cardboard on the floor.

If you run your store on a spreadsheet, you can do all of this with two extra columns and a little discipline: a ceiling per item, and a habit of counting the out and the in-repair alongside the in-stock. Most people stop after a month, not because it is hard but because nothing reminds them. The count that matters is the one that is kept for you.

GearLogs keeps it. Give an item a storage capacity in its form and a small meter appears beside its stock badge: green for what is in stock, blue for what is out on people, olive for anything at the repair bench. Everything you are responsible for counts against the ceiling; a write-off frees the room and a recovery takes it back. Adding stock or registering units past the ceiling is never refused — the first time a change takes you over, the app asks “Record it anyway?”, shows the numbers, and records what you confirm. Administrators hear when an item runs low or goes over, so the person who orders finds out before the person who unpacks.

The storeman still gets sixty radios back on Wednesday. But on Monday the meter said he was holding fifty-eight of a forty-radio shelf, so he ordered none, wrote off the six that were beyond repair, and asked for a second shelf. The boxes stayed in the van.
